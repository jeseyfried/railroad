# Historical sources and methodology

## Important interpretive limits

Iron Roads is a curated historical teaching exhibit. Its interactive lines are **not surveyed tracks**. Each corridor is simplified to straight segments through modern city-center coordinates; the lines are illustrative and may differ considerably from historical rights-of-way. The date for each segment is a representative approximate **"in operation by"** year; in some cases it condenses multiple phases of construction. A single "corridor" can represent portions of predecessor railroads, while different corridors can belong to the same company. Do not use the number of visible records as a measure of historic route mileage or number of railroad companies.

The map intentionally shows a selection of northern and Mid-Atlantic rail developments and omits many other railroads, branches, and stations. Missing lines must **never** be interpreted as evidence of an absence of railroads. The Philadelphia–Baltimore corridor originally required a ferry across the Susquehanna, so the site deliberately does not draw an 1838 rail bridge there. Political boundaries are generalized **modern** boundaries (including West Virginia, which did not exist as a state until 1863), not a reconstructed nineteenth-century base map.

The associated source link for each corridor points to useful historical material on the railroad or relevant region. Some secondary paths and opening dates are generalized for teaching, and **not every reference documents every particular waypoint**. Use company reports, timetables, and period maps for a research-grade exact date/geometry for individual routes.

### Scholarly GIS for further research

The authoritative way to construct a track-level historical atlas is to replace the teaching paths with rigorously researched GIS features. **Jeremy Atack's "Historical Geographic Information Systems (GIS) database of U.S. Railroads"** provides historical railroad shapefiles for 1826–1911, including the `InOpBy` field. It is hosted at Vanderbilt with supporting documentation and methodological qualifications:

- Jeremy Atack, [Historical Transportation SHP files and documentation](https://my.vanderbilt.edu/jeremyatack/data-downloads/).
- NBER, [Transportation Economics in the 21st Century — Rail Data](https://www.nber.org/research/data/transportation-economics-21st-century-rail-data).
- ArcGIS service, [Atack-derived historical railroads layer with geospatial metadata](https://services1.arcgis.com/hVMNhMnY75fwfIFy/arcgis/rest/services/Historic_Railroads_before_1872/FeatureServer).

**This website does not include or claim to reproduce Atack's shapefiles.** Its city-link data is original and interpretive.

## A note on the invention narrative

Railroad technology had multiple points of origin: wooden and iron wagonways predated the steam locomotive. The 1804, 1825, and 1830 milestones represent separate engineering and commercial developments, not one moment of invention. Horse-powered American freight lines and steam common-carrier lines also belong to distinct categories. The railroad's consequences were not uniformly beneficial: construction entailed hazardous labor, contested property rights, environmental changes, and unequal access to new markets.
